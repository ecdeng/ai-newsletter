The argument in one paragraph

Investing is a low-validity domain. Deliberate practice needs well-defined tasks, immediate feedback, repetition and error correction — and stock picking natively supplies none of them. Accumulated practice explains under 1% of performance variance in the professions. And at an information ratio of 0.50, establishing skill at 95% confidence takes 15.37 years: longer than the program, longer than most analyst tenures.

So the design conclusion is

The program cannot validly assess whether a student will make money. Any assessment that tries is grading noise and teaching students to confuse outcomes with decisions. What it can assess rigorously is process fidelity and forecast calibration — and roughly 95% of the assessment volume can be auto-graded at about 5% of the cost, concentrating human judgment on one capstone artefact and its live defence.

On the market simulator

The brief asked whether a full market simulator is worth building. It is not — and the reason is instructive rather than budgetary. Fundamental analysis runs on a quarters-to-years clock; a real-time trading simulator optimises for execution and price feedback, and price feedback is the least valid assessment signal available here. You would spend the most to measure the least.

Build a historical case engine instead: freeze a date, serve only what was knowable then, advance the clock, force a post-mortem against the student's own original thesis. Its value is not realism — it is time compression plus free ground truth.
